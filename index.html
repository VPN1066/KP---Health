Cholesterol has risen sharply

In 20 months, LDL ("bad") cholesterol rose from 118 to 154 mg/dL. Non‑HDL rose from 157 to 190, which the lab classes as "very high", and total cholesterol rose from 205 to 247. This pattern is common after menopause, when falling oestrogen pushes LDL up. South Asian heritage also raises heart risk at any given cholesterol level. The good news is that HDL ("good") cholesterol went up, from 49 to 57.

Ask about: a 10‑year heart risk score (QRISK3), a one-off Lp(a) and an ApoB test, and whether a statin is now advised. Start the food changes on the Plan tab now.

Your plan

Supplements and next steps.

Based on your results and the current evidence. Agree any changes with your GP or pharmacist first.

Swap two, add two.

Your six supplements are mostly sensible. The B complex is clearly doing its job. Vitamin E is the one to drop. The biotin in the collagen (and often in B complexes) can distort thyroid and some heart blood tests. Vitamin D and a soluble fibre such as isabgol are the two additions that fit your results best.

Why it may have risen

  • Menopause. Falling oestrogen raises LDL by roughly 10–20% in the years around and after menopause. That's the most likely driver.
  • Thyroid is not the cause. TSH is normal (2.32), so that's ruled out.
  • Diet. Ghee, butter, coconut oil, paneer, full-fat dairy, fried snacks (farsan) and mithai all push LDL up.
  • Inheritance. Lp(a) is inherited and isn't on a standard lipid panel. A single test tells you whether it adds to your risk.

What's good: HDL is up (49 → 57), triglycerides are slightly down (194 → 181) and inflammation (hs‑CRP 1.72) is only average.

What helps (roughly how much LDL falls)

  • Soluble fibre, 7–10 g a day from oats, isabgol (psyllium), rajma, chana and dal: about 5–10%.
  • Swap saturated fats (ghee, butter, coconut and palm oil) for mustard, groundnut or olive oil: about 5–10%.
  • Plant sterols, about 2 g a day from fortified milk, yoghurt or spreads: about 7–10%.
  • A handful of nuts (30 g almonds or walnuts) every day: about 3–5%.
  • 150 minutes a week of brisk walking, plus strength work twice a week. This mainly raises HDL and lowers triglycerides.

Together, these can lower LDL by about 20–25%. That could bring 154 back to around 115–125. If your risk score is high or Lp(a) is raised, a statin may still be advised, and that's a decision to make with your GP.

Prediabetes is reversible.

An HbA1c of 5.9% means slightly too much sugar has been in your blood over the last 2–3 months. Without changes, about 5–10% of people in this range develop type 2 diabetes each year. With changes, many go back to normal.

One note: large doses of vitamin E can make HbA1c read falsely low (the lab report mentions this). So your true value could be slightly higher, which is one more reason to stop it.

What works best

  • Walk 10–15 minutes after lunch and dinner. This blunts the after-meal sugar spike more than one long walk does.
  • Eat vegetables, dal or protein first, and rice or roti last.
  • Swap white rice and maida for brown rice, millets (jowar, bajra, ragi) or multigrain roti.
  • Cut out sugar in chai, and keep mithai and fruit juice to occasional treats.
  • Strength training twice a week. Muscle is where most blood sugar gets stored.
  • If you're carrying extra weight around the middle, losing even 5% makes a measurable difference.

Protect your bones

After menopause, women can lose up to 2% of their bone density a year. You're already taking calcium, so the gaps are vitamin D (falling) and weight-bearing exercise.

  • Calcium: aim for 1,000–1,200 mg a day in total. Food comes first: dahi, milk, paneer, ragi, sesame, tofu, green leafy vegetables. Then top up with a supplement of no more than 500 mg at a time, taken with a meal.
  • Vitamin D3: 1,000–2,000 IU a day, then recheck after 3 months.
  • Ask about a DEXA bone-density scan if you haven't had one since menopause.
  • Balance work: stand on one leg while brushing your teeth, or try yoga or tai chi. Preventing a fall matters as much as bone density does.

Your protein target

Muscle and bone both need protein. For women over 60, experts recommend 1.0–1.2 g per kg of body weight a day, spread across meals. Move the slider to your weight.

Easy sources: dahi or Greek yoghurt, paneer, tofu, eggs, dal, chana, rajma, sprouts and milk. Collagen powder counts towards your total, but it's an incomplete protein, so it doesn't replace these foods.

Tests to add next time

  • Lipoprotein(a): needed only once in a lifetime. It shows whether inherited cholesterol risk is part of the picture.
  • ApoB: a more precise measure of the particles that cause plaque.
  • Magnesium: you take it, but it has never been measured. It also matters for the QT interval.
  • Folate (and active B12 if your GP agrees): this completes the homocysteine picture.
  • Fasting insulin: shows how hard your body is working to keep sugar normal.

When and how

  • In 3 months: vitamin D (if you start D3), lipid profile and Lp(a)/ApoB.
  • In 6 months: HbA1c, B12, folate, homocysteine and magnesium.
  • Stop biotin for 3 days before any blood test. That includes collagen with biotin and most B complexes. Biotin can make thyroid and some heart test results unreliable.
  • Fast for 10–12 hours (water is fine). Do the test early in the morning, after a normal night's sleep.
  • For urine, use a clean-catch midstream sample. Last time's bacteria were most likely contamination.
